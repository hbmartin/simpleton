The structural problem. Per-language cost runs inverse to evidence strength. The FM oracle — weakest rung, judgment only, no counterexample — is free in all four. Differential fuzzing — the rung that actually produces evidence — is most expensive to port and brutal in Swift. Unmanaged, multi-language support silently pushes you toward weaker oracles while looking like broad coverage. Hence the two commitments everything else serves: capability is declared, never assumed; and every verdict carries its evidence profile rather than a boolean.

The biggest call, and the one most worth arguing with. For rung 7 I chose synthesised differential harnesses over porting native fuzzers per language. The objection is obvious — the harness is now LLM-generated and unverified, which is the exact failure mode Simpleton exists to prevent. What dissolves it is an asymmetry: you never need to trust the harness, only the counterexample, and a counterexample validates itself by replay. A false one dies on re-run; a missed one is coverage loss, already exposed by the eligibility rate. The error mode of a bad harness is silence, not false assurance.

The supporting evidence is stronger than I expected. Cleverest — change-directed LLM regression test generation, taking the commit and diff as input, which is precisely our input — found as many bugs in under 2 minutes as a state-of-the-art directed greybox fuzzer found in 24 hours, across 72 commits to eight real C/C++ projects. That gap is the difference between a rung that fits in CI and one that doesn't. Native fuzzers then become accelerators where they exist rather than prerequisites — and it's the only way Swift gets rung 7 at all.

Two changes to PLAN.md you should weigh:

- Go, not Python, as the first language pack. PLAN's step 1 exists to answer "can rung 7 produce counterexamples on real code, and at what coverage?" Go answers it fastest: native coverage-guided fuzzing, full type info in the stdlib, fast hermetic builds, clean Linux sandboxing — plus Trail of Bits' gosentry fork now swaps in LibAFL with struct-aware and grammar-based fuzzing. Change of language, not of intent.
- Swift's sandbox story doesn't exist. Firecracker and gVisor are Linux. Anything touching Apple frameworks needs macOS runners with process-level isolation — weaker containment, scarcer hardware, no ~125ms boot. That's not a detail to find later: it makes the execution substrate pluggable per ecosystem, and it means a Swift verdict is produced under materially different containment from a Go one. The doc records that in the verdict rather than hiding it.

Where I'm least confident, in order:

1. Rung-7 eligibility on real code. Still the make-or-break number, now four numbers — Python's dynamism, Go's explicit effects, TypeScript's async and Swift's ARC will give genuinely different answers.
2. Whether synthesised harnesses match native fuzzing. Build step 5 exists to test this against Go ground truth. If it fails, Swift's rung 7 is absent rather than degraded, and the four-language promise weakens materially.
3. Whether the FM oracle generalises beyond Java. The 93.8% comes from a Java-only IDE-bug corpus whose authors won't rule out contamination — and because it's the one free rung, it's the one most likely to be over-trusted. Needs separate per-language validation against seeded mutations.

One thing I decided against and should flag: I rejected the universal-IR approach (the Semgrep model of mapping all languages to one AST). It's the most intellectually appealing option and the reason I said no is that Semgrep's own looseness is why it's confined to pattern matching — and Swift's optionals, protocol witnesses and ARC semantics don't survive the mapping. If you think that's wrong, it's the decision that would most change the architecture.
